The Core Idea in 30 Seconds

What Does This Project Actually Do?

An intuitive look at image compression without getting lost in formulas

❌ The Problem: Too Many Numbers!
  • Every pixel is stored independently (4,096 numbers for 64×64).
  • Neighboring pixels are almost identical — extreme redundancy.
  • Storing all 4,096 values consumes unnecessary bandwidth.
✅ The Solution: Keep Top 16 Patterns
  • Find the 16 'Master Patterns' (Eigenvectors).
  • Each pattern has an importance score (Eigenvalue).
  • Keep the top 16, throw away 48 $\rightarrow$ 75% savings!

Step 1: The Input Matrix

How Does a Picture Become Numbers?

From a photo upload to a normalized numerical matrix A

1. Grayscale & Resize
  • Upload any standard image (PNG / JPEG).
  • Convert RGB to Grayscale ($Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B$).
  • Resize to a standard 64 × 64 matrix (4,096 numbers).
  • Normalize: divide by 255.0 so numbers are in $[0.0, 1.0]$.
2. Mean Centering ($\tilde{A} = A - \mu$)
  • Calculate average brightness of each column ($\mu$).
  • Subtract $\mu$ from every pixel value.
  • Why? Centering shifts the coordinate origin to zero so we measure contrast, not room lighting.
🗣️ What to say:
"We take the image, turn it grayscale, and make it a 64×64 grid where 0 is black and 1 is white. Then we subtract the average brightness so our calculations focus on the contrast and features rather than background lighting."

Step 2: The Core Math (Viva Question)

Why Covariance Matrix $C = \tilde{A}^T \tilde{A}$?

The #1 question professors ask in vivas — explained in 3 simple points

1. Always Square

Images are often rectangular ($m \times n$). Rectangular matrices cannot have standard eigenvalues! $C$ is strictly 64 × 64 square.

2. Perfectly Symmetric

$C = C^T$ (mirror matrix). By the Spectral Theorem, symmetry guarantees that all eigenvalues are purely REAL numbers!

3. Perpendicular Axes

Eigenvectors are 100% perpendicular ($90^\circ$ orthogonal). Zero feature overlap, and all eigenvalues are $\ge 0$.

🗣️ What to say:
"Professor, images are rectangular, but eigenvalues only exist for square matrices. The covariance matrix C = A^T A is always square and symmetric. By the Spectral Theorem, symmetry guarantees our eigenvalues are real numbers and the eigenvectors are 100% perpendicular."

Step 3: What Do They Mean?

Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors Made Simple

Think of them as 'Feature Directions' and 'Importance Scores'

Eigenvector ($v_i$) = The Direction
  • Points along a key visual pattern (e.g. jawline, nose shadow, eye contour).
  • All eigenvectors are perpendicular to each other ($v_1 \perp v_2$).
  • Forms a custom coordinate system tailored specifically to this picture.
Eigenvalue ($\lambda_i$) = Importance Score
  • $\lambda_i$ equals the variance (amount of information) along $v_i$.
  • Big $\lambda$ (e.g. 8.4): Major shape, strong lighting, high contrast.
  • Tiny $\lambda$ (e.g. 0.001): Fine noise, minor grain, unnoticeable detail.
🗣️ What to say:
"An eigenvector is an axis or pattern direction in the image, and the eigenvalue is its importance score. The bigger the eigenvalue, the more image details lie along that line. The top 16 eigenvalues carry over 91% of the entire picture's energy."

Step 4: Compressing & Rebuilding

Encoding to Top-16 and Reconstructing

How we compress 4,096 values and rebuild the picture

Compression (Encoding to $k=16$)
  • Select top 16 eigenvectors: $V_{16}$ ($64 \times 16$).
  • Project image: $Z = \tilde{A} V_{16}$ ($64 \times 16$).
  • Instead of 64 columns, store only 16 columns $\rightarrow$ 75% data removed!
Reconstruction (Decoding)
  • Multiply back: $\hat{A} = Z V_{16}^T + \mu$.
  • Eckart-Young Theorem (1936): Proves that NO OTHER 16-number combination can produce a closer match than this!
🗣️ What to say:
"We take the top 16 eigenvectors, project the image onto them (giving 75% compression), and multiply back to reconstruct. By the Eckart-Young theorem, this is provably the best possible 16-component approximation that exists in linear algebra."

Visual Analysis (Very Important!)

Why Does the Reconstructed Image Look Blurred?

The blur is not an error — it is visual proof of data compression!

What Did We Throw Away?
  • We intentionally dropped 48 out of 64 eigenvalues.
  • Dropped eigenvalues = High Frequencies (sharp edges, hair strands, noise).
  • Kept eigenvalues = Low Frequencies (overall face shape, contrast, shadows).
Why This Proves It Works!
  • If the image were 100% sharp, $k=64$ (0% compression!).
  • The blur proves that 75% of the data was discarded.
  • To make it sharper: simply increase $k$ to 32 or 48.
🗣️ What to say:
"Sir/Ma'am, the slight blur is not a defect — it is the visual proof of compression! High eigenvalues hold the broad shape, while low eigenvalues hold sharp micro-details. By discarding the bottom 48 eigenvalues to save space, the image naturally softens. If it wasn't blurred at all, no compression occurred!"

Viva Defense Cheat Sheet

Top 4 Teacher Questions & 1-Line Answers

Keep this slide in mind during your professor's questions!

1. What is an eigenvalue in an image?
Answer: It is the variance (amount of information) along an eigenvector direction. Bigger eigenvalue = more visual shape and contrast captured.
2. Why use np.linalg.eigh instead of eig?
Answer: eigh is specialized for symmetric matrices. It runs 2× faster and guarantees purely real eigenvalues without numerical noise.
3. Can eigenvalues ever be negative here?
Answer: No. Covariance matrix $C$ is Positive Semi-Definite ($x^T C x = \|Ax\|^2 \ge 0$). Therefore, all eigenvalues must be $\ge 0$.
4. Why is the reconstruction blurred?
Answer: Because we deliberately dropped 48 high-frequency eigenvalues. The blur proves compression occurred; keeping all 64 would give 0% compression.
